Algebra 9 — Simultaneous equations (one linear, one non-linear) · Proof

Exercises

Calculator allowed. Marks in brackets. ★ = harder question. Always substitute the linear equation into the non-linear one.

  1. [4]Solve the simultaneous equations $y=x^2-3x+1$ and $y=x+2$, giving your answers to 3 s.f.

    Substitute: $x^2-3x+1=x+2\Rightarrow x^2-4x-1=0$. Formula: $x=\dfrac{4\pm\sqrt{16+4}}{2}=2\pm\sqrt5$, so $x=4.24$ or $x=-0.236$. Then $y=x+2$: $y=6.24$ or $y=1.76$. Solutions $(4.24,\,6.24)$ and $(-0.236,\,1.76)$.

  2. [4]Solve the simultaneous equations $x-y=2$ and $x^2+y^2=20$.

    From the linear equation $y=x-2$. Substitute: $x^2+(x-2)^2=20\Rightarrow2x^2-4x+4=20\Rightarrow x^2-2x-8=0\Rightarrow(x-4)(x+2)=0$. $x=4\Rightarrow y=2$; $x=-2\Rightarrow y=-4$. Solutions $(4,2)$ and $(-2,-4)$ — the two points where the line meets the circle.

  3. [2]Give a counter-example to show that $(x-3)^2=x^2-9$ is false.

    $x=0$: left side $(-3)^2=9$, right side $-9$. Not equal, so the statement is false. (One counter-example is enough.)

  4. [2]Prove that if the difference between two whole numbers is 3, the difference between their squares is a multiple of 3.

    Let the numbers be $n$ and $n+3$. $(n+3)^2-n^2=n^2+6n+9-n^2=6n+9=3(2n+3)$, which is 3 times a whole number.

  5. [4]Prove that the sum of any two consecutive odd numbers is a multiple of 4.

    Any odd number can be written $2n+1$ with $n$ a whole number; the next odd number is $2n+3$. Sum $=(2n+1)+(2n+3)=4n+4=4(n+1)$, a multiple of 4. (Writing "$3+5=8$" is a check, not a proof — the algebra covers every case.)

  6. [9]a Prove that $x^2-6x+10>0$ for all values of $x$.  b Find the minimum point of the graph of $y=x^2-6x+10$.  c Sketch the graph.

    a Complete the square: $x^2-6x+10=(x-3)^2-9+10=(x-3)^2+1$. A square is never negative, so $(x-3)^2\ge0$ and $(x-3)^2+1\ge1>0$ for every $x$.

    b The smallest value, 1, occurs when $(x-3)^2=0$, i.e. $x=3$: minimum point $(3,1)$.

    c U-shaped, vertex $(3,1)$, crosses the $y$-axis at $(0,10)$, never touches the $x$-axis (part a).

Total: 25 marks

Check: substitute the linear into the quadratic/circle, then solve the quadratic — each $x$ gives its own $y$ · a counter-example is a single specific case that fails · proofs use $n$, $2n$ (even), $2n+1$ (odd), consecutive = $n, n+1$ · "always positive": complete the square and argue a square is $\ge0$.
